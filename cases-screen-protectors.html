Cases & Screen Protectors: what actually matters

A case is cheap insurance until you drop your phone on concrete and find out the "military-grade" sticker didn't mean much. Here's what the ratings and materials actually tell you, and where the trade-offs are.

Drop rating

Look for an actual cited test standard (like MIL-STD-810G) rather than just the word "military-grade" on the box — the standard means a specific drop height and surface was tested.

Material

TPU (soft) absorbs shock better but can yellow over time. Polycarbonate (hard) resists scratches better but transfers more impact. Hybrid cases combine both layers.

Screen protector type

Tempered glass protects against scratches and takes the brunt of a drop better than film, but is more likely to crack outright rather than just scuff.

Drop ratings: what the sticker actually means

"Military-grade" isn't a certification body — it's a reference to MIL-STD-810G, a U.S. military testing standard originally meant for equipment, not phone cases. Manufacturers self-report which parts of the standard they tested against, so look for the specific drop height (commonly 4–10 feet) and number of drops rather than the label alone. A case tested to a specific height on concrete is a stronger claim than an untested "rugged" label.

Material trade-offs

Soft TPU cases flex on impact, which absorbs shock well, but can discolor (especially clear cases) after months of UV exposure. Hard polycarbonate resists everyday scuffs and keeps a cleaner look longer, but transmits more shock straight to the phone. Most cases marketed as "protective" combine a soft inner layer with a hard outer shell — that hybrid approach is usually the best balance unless you specifically want an ultra-slim case.

Screen protectors: glass vs. film

Tempered glass is thicker, feels closer to the original screen, and absorbs impact by cracking instead of transferring it to the display underneath — but that means a hard drop can crack the glass even when the phone itself is fine. Film protectors are thinner and less likely to crack outright, but pick up fine scratches faster and don't add meaningful drop protection. For most people, tempered glass is the better default; film makes more sense if you want the case to feel as close to screen-less as possible.
