Power Banks: what actually matters

The mAh number on the box is only half the story — how much of that capacity you actually get to use, and how fast it charges, depends on details most listings bury. Here's what to check.

Usable capacity

Expect to actually use roughly 60–70% of the rated mAh once voltage conversion losses are accounted for. A "20,000mAh" bank won't fully charge a 20,000mAh phone battery.

Output wattage

A high-capacity bank with low output watts will charge your devices slowly. Match the output rating to what your phone or laptop can actually accept.

Pass-through charging

Lets you charge the power bank and a connected device at the same time. Useful for travel; not all banks support it safely.

Why you don't get the full mAh rating

Power banks are rated at their internal cell voltage (usually 3.7V), but they output at 5V or higher to charge your devices — that voltage conversion has real losses. A "10,000mAh" bank typically delivers closer to 6,500–7,000mAh of actual charge to your phone. This isn't false advertising; it's just how the rating convention works. Budget for it when deciding how much capacity you actually need.

Output wattage vs. capacity

A bigger number on the box doesn't mean a faster charge. Capacity (mAh) determines how many charges you get; output wattage determines how fast each charge happens. If you're charging a phone, 18–30W output is typically plenty. If you want to top up a laptop, look specifically for a bank rated 45W or higher via USB-C, since most laptops won't fast-charge below that.

Pass-through charging and portability

Pass-through lets the bank charge a connected phone while it's itself plugged into the wall — handy on a red-eye flight or a hotel with one outlet. Not every bank supports this safely; check the listing specifically rather than assuming. Also weigh capacity against weight: a 26,800mAh bank is genuinely heavy to carry daily, while a 10,000mAh bank fits in a jacket pocket but needs a top-up more often.
